## 2. Examined so far under this syllabus (May 2025, November 2025, May 2026, specimen)

What I could verify:
- Exam sessions May 2025 and November 2025 exist, with SL and HL papers (TZ1, TZ2, TZ3). **med** (from an unofficial mirror listing; not verified against IBO).
- A May 2026 session is referenced by one unofficial file but not confirmed. A file dated May 2026 was flagged as suspicious. **low**.
- No specimen paper was located. **Not verified.**

Question styles that touch Themes A and B (described in my own words; no question text was copied):
- **Multiple choice (1A):** Short stems describing a biological situation (diet, an organelle, a molecule). Distractors often swap a term for a near-synonym or reverse cause and effect. Typical protein-related items: essential vs non-essential amino acids, which organelle synthesises proteins, which bond or interaction gives a structural level. **med**.
- **Data-based (1B and Paper 2 Section A):** Often a sequence comparison (e.g. one amino acid difference between species), a graph or table of enzyme/protein activity against pH or temperature, or a short experimental description. Typical asks: "use the data to", "identify", "state", "suggest". A student must link a data pattern to a structural cause (e.g. a change in R groups changes folding or solubility). **med**.
- **Short answer (Section A):** Asks for a named structure, a bond type, or a brief explanation of how a change affects function. **med**.
- **Extended response (Section B):** Usually one broad prompt that requires linking two or more subtopics (e.g. protein structure to function, or structure to membrane transport). Marks are split into content and a quality mark for organised writing. **low** (see section 1).

### B1.2 Proteins: recurring themes and expected answers

Recurring question patterns (paraphrased):
1. **Amino acid structure.** Expect a generalised drawing or description: central carbon, amino group, carboxyl group, hydrogen, and a variable R group. Mark points typically credit naming the R group as the variable part and stating that R groups differ between amino acids. **med**.
2. **Peptide bond and condensation.** Expect: formed between the carboxyl group of one amino acid and the amino group of another, releasing water (condensation). Mark points usually require the word "condensation" or "water removed". **med**.
3. **Levels of structure.**
   - Primary: the sequence of amino acids. Expect "sequence of amino acids joined by peptide bonds". **high**.
   - Secondary: alpha helix and beta pleated sheet, held by hydrogen bonds between the **C=O of one peptide group and the N-H of another (backbone)**. Expected answers name the atoms or the group, not just "hydrogen bonds". **high** for the concept; the exact atom-level expectation is **med** (seen as a mark point in a mirror mark scheme, unverified).
   - Tertiary: folding held by interactions between R groups, including hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, hydrophobic interactions and, where present, disulfide bridges. **med**.
   - Quaternary: more than one polypeptide chain. Quaternary structure is reported as **HL only** in one source. **med**.
4. **R-group effects.** A recurring idea: variation in R groups affects folding, solubility, and interaction with other molecules. Expected answers link a specific R-group property (charge, polarity, hydrophobicity) to a specific bond or interaction. **med**.
5. **Denaturation.** Expect: change in shape caused by disruption of bonds (hydrogen, ionic, hydrophobic) by heat or pH, with loss of function. Weak answers say "the protein breaks down" or "the enzyme stops working", which do not name the bonds. **med** (general exam practice; IB source not confirmed).
6. **Named examples (Guide: insulin, collagen, haemoglobin, per a secondary summary).**
   - Insulin: a non-conjugated globular protein, hormone; two polypeptide chains joined by disulfide bridges. Expected link: globular shape gives a specific binding site for its receptor. **med** (chain count from general knowledge; not verified in the guide).
   - Collagen: a fibrous, non-conjugated protein with a triple helix; glycine appears at every third position; many hydrogen bonds give strength. Expected link: form (long strands, cross-linked) to function (tensile strength in tendons, skin). **med**.
   - Haemoglobin: a globular, conjugated protein with four polypeptide chains and a haem group containing iron, which binds oxygen. Expected link: quaternary structure and haem group to oxygen transport. **med**.
7. **Essential vs non-essential amino acids (A/B links).** Expected: essential amino acids cannot be synthesised by humans and must come from the diet; non-essential ones can be made from other molecules. Typical context: vegan diets, which must supply all essential amino acids. **med** (seen in a mirror SL mark scheme; unverified).
8. **Translation link.** Expected: ribosome moves along mRNA; amino acids are added one at a time to a growing chain. **med**.

## 3. Common student mistakes (examiner-report style)

Official IB examiner reports were not found. The following come from secondary commentary and from one non-IB examiner report, so treat them as likely patterns, not IB statements.

- Vague wording: "the enzyme stops working", "the protein breaks down". Examiners cannot award the mark without naming the bond or interaction that changes. **med** (secondary commentary; general exam practice).
- Naming "hydrogen bonds" in secondary structure without saying which groups (C=O and N-H) or which parts of the backbone are involved. **med**.
- Saying denaturation breaks **peptide bonds** or covalent bonds. Denaturation breaks weak interactions (hydrogen, ionic, hydrophobic), not the peptide backbone. A non-IB examiner report (Pearson, Edexcel 9BI0, 2023) notes many candidates wrongly break covalent disulfide bonds; **med** as a general pattern, not an IB source.
- Confusing primary structure (sequence) with the folded shape.
- Describing R groups as "the amino acid" or "the side chain" without saying they vary and what property changes.
- Treating fibrous and globular proteins as the same. A form-function answer must link shape to a property (e.g. solubility, strength).
- Using "cell" or "membrane" loosely when asked about a structure at the molecule level.
- For data-based questions: stating a trend without quoting numbers, or not linking the data to the biological cause.
- For Section B: answering one subtopic only, not linking to a second, which misses the quality/linking marks. **low**.

## 5. Official IB command terms (short definitions)

Definitions below are paraphrased from general IB practice and should be checked against the IBO Biology subject guide glossary (the IBO page was unreachable). Confidence: **med** for definitions, **high** that these are real IB command terms.

- **Annotate**: add brief notes to a diagram or graph.
- **Calculate**: obtain a numerical answer, showing working.
- **Comment**: give an opinion or observation about data, based on evidence.
- **Compare**: give the similarities and differences, emphasising similarities (or both).
- **Construct**: make a graph, table, or diagram from given data.
- **Contrast**: give the differences, emphasising differences.
- **Deduce**: reach a conclusion from given information.
- **Define**: give the precise meaning of a term.
- **Demonstrate**: show that something is true or works, with evidence.
- **Describe**: give a detailed account of the features or pattern (what is seen).
- **Design**: plan an investigation or method.
- **Determine**: find a value or answer using a method.
- **Discuss**: give a balanced account of arguments or ideas, including points for and against.
- **Distinguish**: state the differences between two items.
- **Draw**: make a labelled or accurate diagram to scale.
- **Estimate**: give an approximate value.
- **Evaluate**: judge the strengths and weaknesses or the validity of a claim, reaching a conclusion.
- **Explain**: give reasons or a cause-and-effect account (the "because" answer).
- **Identify**: name or select a feature without detail.
- **Label**: add names to a diagram.
- **List**: give a series of points with no explanation.
- **Measure**: obtain a value from an instrument or diagram.
- **Outline**: give a brief account of the main points.
- **Predict**: state an expected outcome based on a principle or data.
- **Resolve**: find a solution to a problem (may be used in data questions).
- **Show**: give enough steps to prove a result.
- **Sketch**: draw a simple, approximately accurate outline.
- **State**: give a short answer with no explanation.
- **Suggest**: propose a possible answer or explanation without a single correct answer.
- **Using the data**: answer using numbers and trends from the given data.
